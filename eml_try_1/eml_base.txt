1. Fundamental definition (axiom)The base Exponent Minus Logarithm (EML) operator takes two arguments and is computed as:$$eml(x, y) = e^x - \ln(y)$$The main rule of the system: only the constant 1 (or the input variable $x$, when building a function) may be used as a starting "building block". Everything else must be derived through recursive nesting of $eml$.2. Derivation of the base constantsTo show how the basis is built, one has to show how the key numbers are obtained from ones.Obtaining the exponent ($e$):$$eml(1, 1) = e^1 - \ln(1) = e - 0 = e$$Obtaining $e^e$ (an intermediate step):Substitute the resulting $e$ into the first argument.$$eml(e, 1) = e^e - \ln(1) = e^e$$Obtaining zero ($0$):Substitute $e^e$ into the second argument of the operator, with one as the first argument.$$eml(1, e^e) = e^1 - \ln(e^e) = e - e = 0$$The full expression of zero through ones only:$$0 = eml(1, eml(eml(1, 1), 1))$$3. Derivation of the base functions (symbolic regression)This is the key section for machine learning and the search for analytic formulas. It shows how $eml$ simulates other mathematical operators.The exponential function ($e^x$):$$eml(x, 1) = e^x - \ln(1) = e^x$$The natural logarithm function ($\ln(x)$):This is the most important proof that the basis works. It requires three nesting steps:Step 1:$$eml(1, x) = e - \ln(x)$$Step 2 (wrap in an exponent):$$eml(eml(1, x), 1) = e^{e - \ln(x)} = \frac{e^e}{e^{\ln(x)}} = \frac{e^e}{x}$$Step 3 (wrap in a logarithm with subtraction from $e$):$$eml(1, \frac{e^e}{x}) = e^1 - \ln\left(\frac{e^e}{x}\right) = e - (e - \ln(x)) = \ln(x)$$The full expression of the natural logarithm function:$$\ln(x) = eml(1, eml(eml(1, x), 1))$$4. Entering the complex plane (trigonometry and $\pi$)The system inevitably enters the complex plane once a negative number reaches the second argument (the logarithm). This makes it possible to extract the geometric constants.The principal branch of the complex logarithm is used: $\ln(-x) = \ln(x) + i\pi$.Generating the imaginary unit and $\pi$:If the algorithm generates $-1$ during mutation (or a directed search), evaluating $eml(0, -1)$ gives a direct route into the complex numbers:$$eml(0, -1) = e^0 - \ln(-1) = 1 - i\pi$$Thanks to this property, a grammar of the form S -> 1 | x | eml(S, S) is able to approximate sines and cosines through Euler's formula ($e^{ix} = \cos(x) + i\sin(x)$), since $eml$ can generate both the exponent and the imaginary components.
1. The gateway to trigonometry: the complex logarithmFor the EML basis to generate periodic functions (sines and cosines), the algorithm must be able to work with negative numbers under the logarithm.Mathematics uses the principal branch of the complex logarithm:$$\ln(-x) = \ln(x) + i\pi$$For the base negative unit ($-1$) the logarithm yields a purely imaginary number:$$\ln(-1) = i\pi$$This gives a fundamental bridge to the imaginary unit ($i$) and the constant $\pi$. For example:$$eml(0, -1) = e^0 - \ln(-1) = 1 - i\pi$$2. The base oscillator (Euler's formula in EML)Once the imaginary unit $i$ appears in the system, EML becomes an ideal wave generator.The first argument of EML works as a pure exponent:$$eml(z, 1) = e^z$$If a purely imaginary number $ix$ is fed into the first argument (where $x$ is the variable, e.g. time or an angle), the operator automatically decomposes into trigonometric components by Euler's formula:$$eml(ix, 1) = e^{ix} = \cos(x) + i\sin(x)$$Consequence for the algorithm:There is no need to assemble a separate complex eml structure for $\sin(x)$ and $\cos(x)$. It is enough to feed $ix$ into the left argument of $eml(ix, 1)$.The real part of the result $\Re(eml(ix, 1))$ is $\cos(x)$.The imaginary part of the result $\Im(eml(ix, 1))$ is $\sin(x)$.3. Classic expression of sine and cosine (through EML)If the architecture requires a strictly analytic expression of sine and cosine through exponents (to stay within the EML graph logic), the direct consequences of Euler's formula are used:Cosine:$$\cos(x) = \frac{e^{ix} + e^{-ix}}{2}$$In EML terms (free constants such as division by $2$ are kept as scaling weights $C=0.5$):$$\cos(x) = C \cdot [eml(ix, 1) + eml(-ix, 1)]$$Sine:$$\sin(x) = \frac{e^{ix} - e^{-ix}}{2i}$$In EML terms (where the constant $C = \frac{1}{2i}$):$$\sin(x) = C \cdot [eml(ix, 1) - eml(-ix, 1)]$$4. Hyperbolic trigonometry (bonus)The hyperbolic functions ($\sinh, \cosh$) are derived even more simply, as they do not require entering the complex plane (no $i$). They are highly useful in neural networks as activation functions.Hyperbolic cosine:$$\cosh(x) = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2}$$In EML terms:$$\cosh(x) = 0.5 \cdot [eml(x, 1) + eml(-x, 1)]$$Hyperbolic sine:$$\sinh(x) = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2}$$In EML terms:$$\sinh(x) = 0.5 \cdot [eml(x, 1) - eml(-x, 1)]$$
